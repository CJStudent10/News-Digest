## 1. Stephen Miller Becomes Trump's New Chief of Staff
`US Headlines` · Covered by 7 outlets

President Trump announced that longtime adviser Stephen Miller will replace Susie Wiles as White House chief of staff, starting Monday. The change comes days after Republicans lost control of Congress in the midterms. Miller is best known as the architect of the administration's strict immigration policies, and Democratic leaders strongly criticized the pick. Wiles will stay on to work on political operations.

**Key facts**
- Miller has been deputy chief of staff for policy since January 2025.
- Wiles was the first woman to serve as White House chief of staff; Axios sources said her exit was a mutual decision driven by the election results.
- Democrats will hold majorities in both the House and Senate in 2027 and plan oversight investigations, including of Miller.
- Separately, a federal judge paused construction of Trump's planned arch near the Lincoln Memorial for 14 days.

**Worth knowing:** The chief of staff shapes how the White House works with the incoming Democratic-led Congress on issues like immigration and cost of living.

## 2. Pope Leo Feels 'Fairly Good' as He Kicks Off a Big South America Trip
`Jamaica` · Covered by 2 outlets

Less than a month after lung cancer surgery, Pope Leo XIV began a 12-day, three-country tour of South America, starting in Uruguay. He told reporters he's in 'fairly good shape' and encouraged people facing cancer not to be afraid and to get regular check-ups. Asked whether he's back to playing tennis, he said, 'Not yet, but I will.'

**Key facts**
- Leo, 71, had part of his right lung removed on October 13 and was discharged October 17.
- The trip includes 12 flights, 36 speeches and 10 cities, including high-altitude Cusco, Peru.
- Uruguay, the first stop, is Latin America's most secular country; only about a third of people there identify as Catholic.

**Good to know**
- Vatican doctors say he is fit to travel and needs no further therapy, and medical care will be on board just in case.

**Worth knowing:** It's a cheerful recovery story with a useful reminder about the value of early detection.

## 3. Mississippi's Senate Race Is Still a Nail-Biter
`US Headlines` · Covered by 3 outlets

Democrat Scott Colom led Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by a little over 2,000 votes as of Friday afternoon, but nobody knows exactly how many ballots are left to count. Mississippi's 82 counties run their own elections and don't have to report to the state until next week, and the state has no recount provisions. A high number of provisional ballots has also drawn attention to how the state maintains its voter rolls.

**Key facts**
- The Colom campaign estimated more than 15,000 provisional ballots; the state GOP chair estimated about 20,000 outstanding ballots.
- The Secretary of State's office said over 9,000 provisional ballots had been processed by midday Friday.
- Absentee ballots postmarked by Election Day can arrive until November 10.

**Good to know**
- The number of outstanding ballots is unknown; estimates come from the campaigns and parties, not official counts.

**Worth knowing:** The result could take a while, and the race is putting a spotlight on how elections are run and voter rolls are managed.

## 8. Novartis Confirms Tumor Reports in Some Zolgensma Patients
`Health & Medicine` · Covered by 2 outlets

Novartis confirmed it has received nine reports of tumors in children treated with Zolgensma, its gene therapy for spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), since 2019. In two cases, there was evidence that the therapy's delivery vector integrated into patients' DNA, which could suggest a link. Patient groups are asking the company for clearer information so families aren't piecing things together from social media.

**Key facts**
- More than 6,000 patients have been treated with Zolgensma.
- 9 tumor reports total; 2 showed vector DNA integration.
- Both of those patients were treated and continue to be monitored.

**Good to know**
- Zolgensma is an FDA-approved therapy (since 2019), not experimental.
- In most cases, no causal link between the therapy and the tumors has been established.
- Tumor risk from vector integration is already listed in the prescribing information; regulators are reportedly reviewing the evidence.
- It's not yet clear whether extra monitoring is recommended for past patients.

**Worth knowing:** Families of treated children may want to talk with their care team, and the findings could shape how the wider gene therapy field monitors safety.
