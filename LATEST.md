## 1. Trump names Stephen Miller White House chief of staff, replacing Susie Wiles
`US Headlines` · Covered by 16 outlets (L 4 · C 9 · R 3)

President Trump announced Friday that longtime adviser Stephen Miller will replace Susie Wiles as White House chief of staff, days after Republicans lost control of the House and Senate. Wiles submitted her resignation Thursday night and will move to Trump's political operation; Trump praised her work and said, "I wanted to make a change." A White House official called the decision "very amicable," while Axios sources described it as mutual but driven by the election results. Miller, currently deputy chief of staff and a key architect of the administration's immigration policies, starts Monday. Democrats, including House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries, sharply criticized the pick.

**Key facts**
- Miller, 41, takes over Monday; Wiles, 69, resigned Thursday night.
- Wiles was the first woman to serve as White House chief of staff and served about 21 months, Trump's longest-serving chief across both terms.
- Trump called Miller "one of my most trusted advisors"; Wiles said serving Trump "has been the honor of a lifetime."
- Jeffries called Miller "a dangerous xenophobe and reckless extremist" and urged Trump to "fire him today."

**How it's being framed**
- **Left:** The Guardian framed the appointment as "extraordinary" and a sign of an aggressive fight with congressional Democrats, describing Miller as Trump's most controversial aide and detailing family separation, the travel ban, the "Hatemonger" biography, and a Democratic letter calling him a white nationalist; it quoted Chris Whipple and David Axelrod as critics. PBS (via AP) was more straightforward, focusing on Wiles's tenure, her cancer recovery and Miller's background.
- **Center:** Axios framed the move as replacing a moderating force with a maximalist one, signaling Trump may double down on policies that it said helped Republicans lose Congress. The Hill focused on Jeffries' denunciation.
- **Right:** Fox News emphasized Trump's and Wiles's warm statements, the "amicable" nature of the change, Wiles's history and cancer recovery, and quoted an administration official calling Miller "a great pick." The Washington Examiner described the move as a defiant, combative response to defeat, comparing it to Stalin's "Not one step back" order.

**Disputed or unclear**
- Whether Wiles left voluntarily: Fox's White House source called it "very amicable" and Axios sources said mutual, while The Guardian described her as "ousted."
- How Miller will approach the incoming Democratic Congress; commentators predict confrontation, but this is speculation.

**Why it matters:** The choice of chief of staff signals how the White House intends to deal with a newly Democratic Congress for the next two years.

## 3. Voters back data center limits; White House says AI build-out will continue
`AI & Cybersecurity` `US Headlines` · Covered by 5 outlets (L 2 · C 2 · R 1)

Voters supported restrictions on data center development in nearly all local ballot measures this election, and many winning Democratic candidates pledged tougher regulation. National Economic Council Director Kevin Hassett said Friday he still expects data center growth to continue as projected, arguing near-term projects are unaffected and that welcoming states will keep building. Trump wrote that he is "100% in favor of Super Intelligence" and will work with states that want data centers. Meanwhile, OpenAI announced it would stop using non-disclosure agreements with public officials on data center projects, and incoming Democratic officials such as Wisconsin Gov.-elect David Crowley are weighing moratoriums and disclosure rules.

**Key facts**
- Ballotpedia: majorities voted to restrict data centers in at least 46 of roughly 50 measures this year.
- AdImpact: more than $380 million spent on TV ads mentioning data centers; Democrats outspent Republicans more than 3-to-1.
- Data Center Watch: 32 of 34 winning governors support stronger regulation or restrictions; 12 support bans or moratoriums.
- CNBC Voter Poll: 56% of voters called data centers a crucial or major issue.

**How it's being framed**
- **Left:** The Verge and PBS (AP) emphasized a coming tougher regulatory environment, Democratic wins in data-center hot spots, concerns about water, power bills and the environment, and industry responses such as OpenAI dropping NDAs. AP noted data centers were not a top issue for most voters.
- **Center:** CNBC centered Hassett's optimistic White House view and linked Thursday's market plunge to political uncertainty; TechCrunch focused on what the outcome means for the tech industry.
- **Right:** The Daily Caller cast the results as a populist rebuke of "tech oligarchs" rather than a byproduct of the Democratic wave, highlighting cross-partisan opposition including 58% of Republicans and the industry's spending advantage.

**Disputed or unclear**
- Tallies differ slightly: TechCrunch says 40 of 41 Tuesday measures passed; Daily Caller says 46 of 47 decided; Ballotpedia via CNBC says at least 46 of about 50.
- How important data centers were to voters: CNBC and Daily Caller cite polls ranking it highly, while AP says it was not a top issue for most voters.
- Whether build-out will actually slow, as Hassett disputes.

**Why it matters:** The fate of the data center boom affects electricity bills, local land and water use, and a major driver of US economic growth and stock markets.

## 4. US adds 165,000 jobs in October, beating forecasts
`US Headlines` · Covered by 5 outlets (L 1 · C 2 · R 2)

Employers added 165,000 jobs in October, nearly three times the roughly 59,000 economists expected, and unemployment edged down to 4.1% from 4.2%, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Friday. August and September were revised up by a combined 84,000 jobs. Wages rose 3.1% from a year earlier, still below recent inflation, and a broader underemployment measure rose. October's consumer price report is due Tuesday.

**Key facts**
- Unemployment rate: 4.1%, down from 4.2%.
- Professional and business services added 70,000 jobs; health care 42,000; transportation and warehousing 25,000; manufacturing 19,000.
- U-6 underemployment rose from 7.6% to 7.9%; part-time for economic reasons rose by 339,000 to 4.8 million.
- Average hourly earnings up 3.1% year over year; September inflation was 3.7%.

**How it's being framed**
- **Left:** The Guardian highlighted labor-market resilience but stressed that wages trail inflation, the Iran war's effect on gas prices, Fed rate hikes and voter frustration with the economy expressed in the midterms.
- **Center:** WBTV and The Hill reported the numbers straightforwardly, with WBTV noting the rising U-6 rate and wages below inflation.
- **Right:** The Daily Caller called the report a welcome rebound but cited economists warning of persistent weakness, especially for entry-level workers and the long-term unemployed. Fox Business emphasized that the gains "blew past" expectations.

**Why it matters:** The jobs data shapes Federal Reserve rate decisions and how households experience the economy amid high prices.
