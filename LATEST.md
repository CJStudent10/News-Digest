## 1. Fire at Indonesian candy factory kills at least three; dozens missing
`Asia Business & Politics` · Covered by 5 outlets (L 1 · C 3 · R 1)

A fire broke out Saturday afternoon at the Fonusa Agung Mulia confectionery factory in Serang district, Banten province, about 50km west of Jakarta. At least three deaths are confirmed and dozens of workers remain unaccounted for. Police removed 24 body bags, which police said may contain remains of more than one person. Police say the victims cannot be identified visually and are collecting DNA from relatives, a process Fox News reported could take two to three weeks. The cause is under investigation, though survivors said the fire began in a cooling unit on the third floor, and the search was paused overnight and set to resume Monday.

**Key facts**
- At least three people confirmed dead; 24 body bags recovered as of Sunday.
- Four injured people were taken to a hospital and a clinic.
- 15 fire trucks were deployed, alongside police, military, Red Cross and volunteers.
- The factory makes Milkita candies and is linked to Indonesian food company UNIFAM (United Family Food).
- Firefighters said the building was already engulfed when they arrived, so they could not tell whether its fire protection system had worked.

**How it's being framed**
- **Left:** The Guardian ran a straight wire-style account, emphasising the rescue effort, relatives waiting at the gate, the fire official's comment on the protection system, and Indonesia's record of deadly fires.
- **Center:** DW, CNA and the BBC focused on casualty counts, rescue operations and official quotes. DW and the BBC highlighted the 24 body bags and the expected rise in the death toll. DW explicitly raised questions about workplace safety standards.
- **Right:** Fox News gave the most detailed breakdown of worker numbers from the company, stressed the grim identification process and reignition risk, and also framed the fire as part of ongoing concern over workplace safety in Indonesia.

**Disputed or unclear**
- The number missing varies: 36 (CNA, Guardian, BBC), 37 (DW), 29 missing plus 8 unconfirmed (Fox, citing the factory), and about 50 unaccounted for (the factory owner, per Fox).
- Estimates of how many were inside range from about 60 (the governor) to 65 in the affected area out of about 300 on site (the company).
- Rescued survivors were given as 11 (DW) or 15 (BBC).
- The cause is officially under investigation; the cooling-unit origin comes from survivors' accounts.

**Why it matters:** The final death toll could be far higher than three, and the fire adds to scrutiny of industrial safety in Indonesia.

## 2. COP31 climate talks end without main agreement after dispute over fossil fuel language
· Covered by 8 outlets (L 4 · C 3 · R 1)

UN climate talks in Antalya, Turkey, ran nearly two days past their Friday deadline. They ended Sunday without the overarching 'cover decision' after the EU, UK, Panama, the Marshall Islands, Norway and others rejected a draft that did not mention fossil fuels. The draft, from the joint Turkish-Australian presidency, recalled the 2023 Dubai agreement to transition away from fossil fuels and set a goal of capturing and storing 1 billion metric tons of carbon. Russia and Saudi Arabia pushed to keep fossil fuels out of the text. Smaller decisions, including one on a 'just transition' for workers, were adopted, and UN Secretary-General António Guterres said he was 'deeply disappointed.'

**Key facts**
- The draft cover decision required near-unanimous consensus and was not adopted.
- EU spokesman Darragh O'Brien, Ireland's minister: 'It's unconscionable that we would have a climate act that makes no reference to fossil fuels.'
- The 10-page draft did not include the words fossil, oil, coal or phase out.
- The talks ran roughly 44–48 hours overtime, which the AP describes as the longest ever.
- Australia's Chris Bowen said the Dubai commitment remains in force and the energy transition is 'irreversible.'

**How it's being framed**
- **Left:** The Guardian framed the result as a dramatic rejection, quoting O'Brien saying the EU would not accept a deal that 'greenwashes fossil fuels' and calling it a 'line in the sand'. It also quoted Power Shift Africa's Mohamed Adow accusing Europe of hypocrisy. PBS carried the AP account, adding context on accelerating warming, the Iran-war energy shock and El Niño.
- **Center:** The Korea Herald (AP) focused on procedural chaos: the three-hour objection window, the African Group's complaint and Kurum's hot-mic remark to Simon Stiell. DW highlighted developing-country campaigners blaming rich nations for blocking transition finance, and gave Russia's and Saudi Arabia's reasoning. The Hill led with Guterres's disappointment.
- **Right:** The Washington Examiner ran the same AP account as PBS, with nearly identical framing: the fossil fuel dispute, Russian and Saudi opposition, criticism of the presidencies, and Bowen's insistence the transition is happening.

**Disputed or unclear**
- Who bears blame is contested: some point to petrostates such as Russia and Saudi Arabia, while others, including campaigners quoted by DW and the Guardian, fault rich nations that expand fossil fuels and withhold transition finance.
- The AP reported Kurum was absent when the failure was announced, while the Guardian quoted him acknowledging there was no consensus.
- Carbon capture and storage remains disputed: Saudi Arabia says 'all solutions' are needed, while critics say it prolongs fossil fuel use.

**Why it matters:** The breakdown leaves unclear whether the 2023 pledge to move away from fossil fuels will gain momentum as warming nears the Paris 1.5C threshold.

## 5. South Korea's education minister resigns over last-minute anti-AI cheating measures for college exam
`Korea` `AI & Cybersecurity` · Covered by 4 outlets (L 1 · C 3 · R 0)

Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin resigned Sunday, and President Lee Jae Myung accepted. The resignation followed criticism of hastily imposed rules meant to stop AI-assisted cheating on the Suneung college entrance exam held Thursday. The measures followed a confession by the top scorer in a national tax accountancy exam that he had used a hidden camera, a smartphone and AI. They included pencil-only rules, stickers over clothing buttons, blocked public Wi-Fi, and three nationwide emergency phone alerts during the exam. Choi said he could not escape criticism that the ministry had failed to prepare. Prime Minister Han Seong-sook was told to handle follow-up measures, and a successor will be nominated.

**Key facts**
- 111 suspected cases of cheating involving electronic devices were detected; nearly half were found through the emergency alerts (CNA).
- About 500,000 students took the Suneung (BBC).
- Asked in parliament whether he was confident cheaters could be caught, Choi, 72, said he was not, citing his age.
- It was the first time South Korea used its national disaster alert system to catch exam cheaters (BBC).

**How it's being framed**
- **Left:** The Guardian detailed the crackdown, including a reward of at least $75,000 for reporting cheating, and noted that teachers' unions called the response belated. It set the story in the context of how central the exam is to Korean life.
- **Center:** Yonhap, the BBC and CNA reported the resignation and the measures in similar terms. Yonhap also noted that a perfect scorer on the law school entrance test was found dead after cheating allegations. The BBC highlighted the anxiety honest test takers felt, for example about their glasses.

**Disputed or unclear**
- Police are still investigating cheating allegations tied to the law school entrance exam case.

**Why it matters:** The episode shows how AI tools are straining high-stakes testing and how governments may struggle to respond.
