## 1. Binance says fake job interviews and an internal AI tool enabled record crypto theft
`AI & Cybersecurity` `Korea` · Covered by 11 outlets (L 2 · C 8 · R 1)

Binance published a postmortem on Tuesday describing how attackers stole roughly $6.3–6.4 billion from the exchange in what outlets call the largest crypto theft to date. The company said the multi-stage campaign began in June, when an engineer downloaded a tampered installer for an AI tool. It continued in September, when a second engineer was lured through fake job interviews into running malicious code on his work computer. The attackers then abused an internal AI tool and stolen credentials and launched the theft on October 29. The FBI has attributed the attack to a North Korean unit called TraderTraitor, and Binance says customers will not bear the losses.

**Key facts**
- The FBI attributed the theft to a North Korean state-linked unit known as TraderTraitor.
- Binance says the intrusion began in June 2026 and the theft was launched on October 29 between 17:15 and 19:56.
- CEO Richard Teng said 98% of affected tokens have been replenished and the rest are being procured; about $2.85 billion has been frozen or intercepted.
- Teng told Fox Business that "this should not have happened" and that the company bears full responsibility.
- Binance says it has rebuilt affected systems and commissioned an independent cybersecurity review.

**How it's being framed**
- **Left:** NPR told the story through the engineer who thought he was job-hunting and quoted Binance's apology. The Verge reported the technical details but questioned why such a well-known tactic still worked, and noted how little Binance disclosed about the internal AI tool.
- **Center:** The Hacker News gave a detailed technical timeline drawn from Binance's report. Axios framed the theft as part of a wider North Korean strategy of exploiting both sides of the job market. The Hill gave a brief summary.
- **Right:** Fox Business centered on the interview with Teng, stressing that customers lose nothing, Binance's large reserves, and a return to normal operations.

**Disputed or unclear**
- Outlets give different totals: The Hacker News says $6.43 billion, others say $6.3 billion, and Fox says more than $6 billion.
- Binance has given few details about the internal AI tool and why it could be misused.
- The Verge reports the first malware spread through a malicious Google ad; Binance's account as quoted refers only to a search engine.
- The timeline relies largely on Binance's own account.

**Why it matters:** It shows how social engineering combined with AI tools can lead to theft on an unprecedented scale, with implications for any company whose staff are job-hunting online.

## 5. Oklahoma man charged over hundreds of white-powder letters sent before elections
`US Headlines` · Covered by 3 outlets (L 1 · C 1 · R 1)

Federal authorities charged Oklahoma City resident Robin Martin with conveying a hoax and mailing threatening communications. He is accused of sending more than 700 letters containing white powder to election officials, members of Congress, pollsters, media and law enforcement ahead of the 2024 and 2026 elections. The powders tested so far were flour or yeast, though not all letters have been tested. Martin was arrested Monday and was expected in federal court in Oklahoma City on Tuesday.

**Key facts**
- The letters' return address read "U.S.T.E.A., United States Traitor Elimination Army."
- The letters warned of arrests for treason and, according to an FBI affidavit, claimed Trump won the 2020 election.
- The FBI cites video of Martin's car at mailboxes and his fingerprints on a letter addressed to FBI Director Kash Patel.
- The mailings led to evacuations of election offices in Louisiana and Oklahoma.

**How it's being framed**
- **Left:** PBS/AP gave a detailed account of the evidence and included the affidavit's description of the letters' 2020 election claims.
- **Center:** The Hill gave a brief factual summary of the charges.
- **Right:** The Washington Examiner stressed that the recipients were bipartisan and placed the case among recent hoax-letter incidents. It did not mention the letters' claims about the 2020 election.

**Disputed or unclear**
- Not all letters have been tested.
- Martin had no attorney listed and has not responded to the charges publicly.

**Why it matters:** The case shows the threats and disruption facing election officials during voting periods.

**Disputed or unclear**

**Why it matters:** Regional conflict is disrupting major sporting events and calling into question Saudi Arabia's role as a host.
